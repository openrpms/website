About RPMS

The Resource and Patient Management System is the health information system of the Indian Health Service. This page is a short introduction for anyone new to it. Terms in bold are explained in the Glossary.

What it is

RPMS is one system that covers both the clinical and the business side of a health facility. It holds the patient chart, orders, labs, pharmacy, and immunizations. It also handles registration, scheduling, billing, and the reports a facility owes.

IHS describes it as more than 50 software applications that work together. Each facility runs its own copy, so a small clinic and a large hospital can each work on their own terms. Data is sent upward on a schedule for national reporting.

IHS has built and maintained RPMS for more than 40 years, and it continues to release updates.

Who uses it

RPMS serves American Indian and Alaska Native patients. It runs in facilities operated by IHS, by tribes, and by urban Indian health programs. Providers, nurses, pharmacists, registration clerks, coders, and billing staff all work in it every day.

How it fits together

RPMS has two halves. The server holds the data and the programs that act on it. Staff reach the server through programs on their own computers.

  • The server is written in M (also called MUMPS) and stores its records with FileMan. It runs on a database engine: InterSystems IRIS in IHS facilities, or the open-source YottaDB.
  • The programs staff open include VueCentric for the chart, iCare for a panel of patients, and BPRM for the front desk. Many staff also use the original text screens directly.

Architecture goes into the layers, and Applications covers the programs staff open, and Packages lists the packages on the server.

Its relationship to VistA

RPMS and the VA's VistA are close relatives. They share core packages, including FileMan, Kernel, and MailMan, which IHS and VA developers built together. Above that shared base, each system has its own applications for the people it serves.

What is public

RPMS was written by federal employees, so it is in the public domain. IHS publishes packages and patches, and the code has been released through FOIA requests for decades. A few parts are withheld, such as licensed code sets and some controlled-substance reporting.

The public files are the pieces of a working system, not a ready-to-run system. The last complete public database is from 2021, and everything since arrives as patches. Some desktop programs also depend on commercial libraries that are not part of the release.

Licensing covers what is public domain and what is not. How a release is built covers how the public files become a working system, and what is still missing.

Where things stand

IHS is building a new enterprise electronic health record, called PATH EHR (Patients at the Heart). General Dynamics Information Technology is building it, using Oracle Health technology. The Lawton Service Unit in Oklahoma is the pilot site. IHS's program page lists a September 2026 go-live target for the pilot. Federal, tribal, and urban facilities are to follow in later groups, and IHS has not published dates for those.

Until a facility moves, it keeps running RPMS. That makes RPMS worth understanding for years yet, and its decades of design worth carrying forward either way.

What this site does

OpenRPMS works from what IHS has made public. The FOIA files go in, and working images come out, so anyone can stand up a running RPMS to learn from, test against, or build on.

The site looks at RPMS in three layers, each a different perspective on the same system.

  • Users: the people who work in RPMS, and what they need it to do.
  • RPMS: the data and core logic, built from the FOIA files, and the RPCs it answers. This is the core of OpenRPMS.
  • Applications: the interfaces and views people work in, built on those RPCs.
